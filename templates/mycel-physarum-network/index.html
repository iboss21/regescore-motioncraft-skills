The method

Borrowed from a slime mold.

Physarum polycephalum can solve a maze and rebuild a rail map of a country. Mycel turns that behavior into a routing engine that explores, reinforces and prunes in continuous time.

01 / SEED

Drop the hubs

Warehouses, ports and depots become glowing food sources. Capacity, windows and cost are encoded as how brightly each one calls.

02 / FORAGE

Release the swarm

Forty thousand agents wander, sense the trail ahead and lay more of it. Good paths are walked more, so they get stronger.

03 / PRUNE

Keep what survives

Unused trail evaporates. What remains is a lean network with sensible redundancy, ready to be exported as lanes, loads and ETAs.

The platform

Everything a dispatcher would ask for.

The swarm is the engine. Around it sits the tooling a real operations room needs: constraints, explanations, carbon accounting and an API that stays out of the way.

Disruption healing

Close a road, flood a depot or slash a ferry timetable. The network rebuilds around it in seconds, not in the next planning cycle.

Constraint aware

Driver hours, cold-chain windows, axle limits and customs hold times shape the field instead of being bolted on afterwards.

Carbon ledger

Every grown path carries a per-ton emissions figure, so greener alternatives show up as a bright second route, not a report.

Explainable paths

Scrub back through the growth and see exactly why a lane was kept, how often it was walked and what would have replaced it.

Boringly good API

REST and streaming endpoints, typed clients and webhooks. Push in hubs and demand, pull out lanes, loads and ETAs.

Digital twin

Fork the live network, test a new depot or a fuel surcharge on a sandbox swarm, and compare the regrown map side by side.
